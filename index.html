Why Dog Tag Day Exists

The uniform is gone. The sacrifice is not.

Dog tags may rest in a drawer, holding a name, a story, or the memory of someone who never came home.

On April 18, we bring those stories into the light.

When you see the tags, look at the person and let them know: “I see you. I remember.”

Exactly What Happens on Dog Tag Day

What do I physically do on April 18?

Participate wherever you are. No ticket, meeting, or special location is required.

1

Wear the tags

If you served, put on or display your military dog tags.

2

Honor someone

Family members may wear or display the tags of someone they love or remember.

3

Acknowledge them

If you see dog tags, recognize the person — a nod, handshake, or simply, “I see you.”

4

Share the story

Take a photo or video, tell the story behind the tags, and invite others to do the same.

Our Mission

Don’t let our veterans fade into the background.

Dog Tag Day is a national movement built around one simple act of visibility. Veterans and service members are invited to wear or display their dog tags. Families may wear tags in honor or memory of someone they love. Those stories deserve to be seen, heard, and remembered.

A Year-Round Veteran Service Program

Veteran Visibility & Connection Program

Dog Tag Day is more than one day of recognition. We are building a nationwide program that preserves veterans’ stories, reconnects veterans with their communities, and guides those who request help toward trusted local services. April 18 opens the door; the work continues all year.

1

Preserve Their Stories

Record veteran interviews, photographs, and service histories—with permission—and protect them in a growing public archive for families and future generations.

2

Train Local Organizers

Equip community volunteers with event guides, story-recording standards, outreach materials, and clear procedures for serving veterans respectfully.

3

Connect Veterans to Help

When a veteran asks for assistance, provide a warm connection to verified local resources for benefits, housing, food, transportation, employment, or mental health.

4

Build April 18 Events

Help communities, schools, employers, veterans groups, and civic organizations hold meaningful Dog Tag Day recognition and story-preservation events.

5

Follow Up Personally

Reconnect with participating veterans after referrals and events to learn whether they received help, made a lasting connection, or need another pathway.

6

Report the Results

Publish annual totals showing veterans recognized, stories preserved, events held, partnerships created, referrals completed, and veterans who confirmed receiving help.

PHASE 1 — NEVADA PILOTBuild the operating model in Las Vegas and surrounding communities: recruit and train volunteers, record stories, hold events, establish referral partners, and document results.
PHASE 2 — MULTI-STATE EXPANSIONProvide tested toolkits, training, equipment, and local support to additional communities through qualified veteran and civic partners.
PHASE 3 — NATIONAL NETWORKCoordinate April 18 participation, story preservation, resource connections, public reporting, and year-round follow-up across the country.

Proposed first-year outcomes

With sufficient funding and qualified partners, the Nevada pilot will work toward these measurable targets. They are goals—not claims of work already completed.

1,000Veterans recognized and engaged
500Stories or interviews preserved
25Community recognition events
100Volunteers and partners trained
250Verified assistance connections
100%Referrals receiving follow-up

What a major investment would build

  • People: veteran-resource navigators, program coordination, volunteer training, and follow-up.
  • Direct community work: local event support, accessibility, transportation assistance, and small partner awards.
  • Story preservation: mobile video and audio equipment, production, consent management, and secure archiving.
  • National infrastructure: community toolkits, partner support, outreach systems, data collection, and independent evaluation.

Our commitment: every funded activity must produce a visible service, a documented connection, a preserved story, or a measurable result for veterans and their families. Annual results and the use of funds will be reported publicly.

The People Behind the Tags

Help America see who served.

A veteran may live two doors away and still feel invisible. A family may hold a set of tags—and a story no one has ever asked to hear. A service member may come home, remove the uniform, and watch the sacrifice disappear from public view.

Dog Tag Day changes that. On April 18, the tags become visible—and so do the lives, courage, grief, pride, and families behind them.

We need sponsors, partners, and funders who will help carry that recognition into communities across America. Your support helps preserve stories, reach families, equip local organizers, and build a national moment that tells every veteran: You are seen. You are remembered. You still matter.

Veteran Identity

The Uniform Comes Off. The Training Doesn’t.

Military service changes how people communicate, organize, respond, and look after those beside them.

Years later, a veteran may still arrive early, study every exit, prepare for every possibility, and speak a language learned in uniform.

That isn’t automatically trauma. It is training, identity, discipline, and belonging—and it does not disappear when military service ends.

PCS • MOS • SITREP • POG • BOHICA

The words remain. The habits remain. The stories remain.

On April 18, the dog tags say, “I served.”
A fist to the heart answers, “I see you.”
