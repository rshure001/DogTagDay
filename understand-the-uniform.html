Understand the Uniform:
Life After Service

Military service can shape a person's teamwork, identity, routine, and sense of belonging. Understanding what service—and the transition home—can mean helps communities recognize veterans with respect.

What the public can learn

Dog Tag Day Foundation is developing educational workshops and community programs for the public, schools, families, civic groups, veterans, and community organizations.

1. Life in the Military

Explore teamwork, discipline, responsibility, shared purpose, and the bonds formed through service.

2. The Meaning Behind Dog Tags

Learn about dog tags as symbols of identity, service, remembrance, and connection.

3. When the Uniform Comes Off

Understand how leaving service can change a person's routine, team, role, and sense of purpose.

4. The Struggles We Don’t Always See

Learn how isolation, stress, or difficulty adjusting may be hidden—and how to listen without judgment.

5. From Understanding to Action

Practice respectful ways to acknowledge veterans and connect people with appropriate support.

Programs may include educational materials, moderated discussion, questions and answers, and veteran or qualified community speakers when available. Personal stories are shared only with permission. No veteran is required to explain their service or tell their story.

12 questions that build understanding

1. What is military life really like?

It often centers on teamwork, discipline, sacrifice, and relying on the people beside you.

2. What happens when a veteran takes off the uniform?

They may lose a familiar team, daily routine, and clear sense of purpose while adjusting to civilian life.

3. Why can coming home be harder than people realize?

Because the structure, community, and mission of service can change all at once.

4. What do civilians misunderstand most?

Some effects of service are invisible, and each veteran’s experience is different.

5. How can a veteran look fine but still be struggling?

People can hide pain; appearance alone cannot tell us how someone is doing.

6. Why might a veteran feel isolated after service?

Leaving service can mean losing everyday connection, routine, and belonging.

7. What if a veteran feels alone or doesn’t know where to turn?

Listen without judgment, stay connected, and help them find appropriate support.

8. Can losing connection and purpose contribute to suicidal thoughts?

Yes. Asking directly and compassionately can open a path to help.

9. What can we do if we think a veteran may be in crisis?

Talk openly, listen, stay with them if there is immediate danger, and connect them with crisis support. In the U.S., call or text 988; press 1 for the Veterans Crisis Line.

10. What should you say when a veteran tells you they’re struggling?

“I’m glad you told me. I’m listening. How can I support you?”

11. How can families, employers, and communities help?

Listen, be patient, offer meaningful opportunities, and help people find trusted resources when needed.

12. How can you participate in Dog Tag Day?

On April 18, veterans who choose may wear or display their dog tags. Everyone can help spread awareness and respectfully acknowledge veterans—such as with a wave, a greeting, or Dog Tag Day’s fist-to-heart gesture. Participation and sharing personal stories are always voluntary.

Understanding should lead to recognition.

Dog Tag Day is observed every April 18. Help make veterans visible—not only on one day, but throughout the year.
